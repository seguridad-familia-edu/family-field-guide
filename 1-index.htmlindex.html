Scams & Spin: a family field guide

One sitting, no jargon, no shame. The new phone scams and the new fake news, shown to you piece by piece — so they can never be run on you fresh.

If you have a phone, a family you love, and a little savings, you are the target market of a new industry — not because you are careless, but because you are worth targeting. Two things changed at once: artificial intelligence can now copy any voice or face from a few seconds of audio or a single photo, and much of the “news” in your feed is now written, voiced, or staged by machines that get paid whenever you stay angry and keep scrolling.

This guide takes an unusual approach: it shows you safe fakes, up close, here where they cannot hurt you. Psychologists call it inoculation — like a vaccine, a small controlled dose of a trick teaches your eye and ear to catch the real thing later. It is not folklore. It is a research program at the University of Cambridge, tested with Google on more than 30 million YouTube viewers, and it measurably makes people harder to fool. Every claim on this page links to its source at the bottom.

Before we start: nobody is immune to this — not professors, not retired agents, not your kids. The people running these operations are professionals with scripts, training, and psychology research of their own. Falling for a well-built con says nothing about your intelligence. The only shameful move in this whole field belongs to the scammer.

Part 1 — The phone that lies

1. Any voice can now be copied from a short sample

Security researchers at McAfee found that a scammer needs only a short sample of someone's voice — a posted video, a podcast clip, a voicemail greeting — to generate a convincing clone. In their 2023 survey of 7,000 adults across seven countries, 1 in 10 people said they had already been contacted by an AI voice clone of someone they knew, and of those who fell for it, 77% lost money. Seven in ten said they could not confidently tell a cloned voice from the real one.

So we will not teach you to tell them apart — soon nobody can. We will teach you something better: never trust a voice alone. Trust the process. The process is in sections 7 and 8.

2. Hear them for yourself: four scam calls, every one fake

Each recording below was generated by AI for this lesson. AI DEMO None of these people exist. Each clip opens with a spoken notice and ends with a safety note — the tool we used requires that. A real scammer will never give you either. Under each recording is the trap: the exact sentence where the con lives.

Call 1 — "The bank fraud department" AI DEMO

59 seconds

What it says: "What you are about to hear is a demonstration… This is Michael calling from the fraud prevention department of your bank… a wire transfer for $2,400… Your phone should have just received a text with a six-digit security code. Please read that code to me now."

The trap is the code. No bank ever asks you to read a security code aloud. That code is a key to your account — and the bank itself just sent it to you. Whoever gets it, gets in. A real fraud department can block a transfer without you reading anything. Hang up. Call the number on the back of your card.

Call 2 — "The grandson in trouble" AI DEMO

58 seconds

What it says: "Grandma, it's me… I was in a car accident last night. I broke my nose, that's why I sound funny… Please don't tell mom and dad… A courier will come to the house for the money."

The trap is secrecy plus urgency plus a stranger at your door. A real grandchild in real trouble can answer one question only they would know — or the family safe word (section 8). You are not abandoning them by checking. You are protecting them.

Call 3 — "The kidnapping" AI DEMO

58 seconds — two parts played back to back: first a terrified voice crying for its mother, then "the kidnapper."

What it says: "Mommy, Mommy, help me! They took me…" then a man's voice: "Do not hang up. Do not call the police… You are going to get in your car and drive to your bank right now."

The trap is the one phone call this call is designed to stop you from making. This con is called virtual kidnapping. The FBI warns that criminals now combine it with cloned voices and fake "proof of life" photos assembled from your own social media. In most cases there is no victim at all. Break contact. Call your family member yourself. Call the police. One minute of checking collapses the whole story.

Call 4 — "The government warrant" AI DEMO

66 seconds

What it says: "…connected to drug trafficking and money laundering activity in the state of Texas. A federal arrest warrant has been issued under your name… To speak with a federal agent… press 1 or stay on the line." Then a live "agent" asks for your name, date of birth, and the last four digits of your Social Security number.

The trap is that real agencies never work this way. No arrest is announced by robocall. No warrant gets "resolved" over the phone with your personal details. This script is mass-dialed by the millions because fear works on a few percent of everyone it reaches. Hang up. If you want certainty, find the agency's official number and call it yourself.

4. The six-digit trick

A text arrives from a relative: "I typed your number by accident — can you send me the code you just got?" Or the caller from the "bank" asks you to confirm the code they just sent. Here is what is actually happening: they are sitting on the login or password-reset page of YOUR account — WhatsApp, Google, your bank — and the code landing on your phone is the only thing between them and everything inside. The FTC is blunt about it: only a scammer asks you to share a verification code. Hand it over and the takeover runs outward in your name: your children get a message "from you" asking for money, and they trust it, because it is you.

Rule of thumb: a login code is a house key that works once. You would not read the teeth of your house key to a stranger on the phone. Never read, forward, or type a code because someone asked — whoever they sound like.

5. The gift-card rule — and the other tells that never have an exception

The Federal Trade Commission's phrasing: only scammers tell you to buy a gift card to pay them. No bank, court, IRS agent, utility, sheriff, or bail bondsman accepts gift cards, ever, for anything. Gift cards are demanded because the codes can be drained in seconds and traced by no one. The same logic covers cryptocurrency ATM deposits, wire transfers to a "safe account," gold bars handed to a courier, and any "refund" process that ends with you moving money. Whenever the payment method's whole point is that the money cannot be recovered, unrecoverable is the point.

6. The numbers, and two true stories

$16.6 billion — what Americans reported losing to internet crime in 2024, according to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center. People over 60 lost the most of any age group: nearly $5 billion, and filed the most complaints. And that is only what gets reported. Shame keeps most of it invisible.

She believed she was working for the FBI. Judith Boivin, an 80-year-old Maryland retiree — a nurse, social worker, and psychotherapist who spent her whole career caring for others — spent months believing she was secretly assisting federal agents. The con took $600,000, her life's savings. Told in the Washington Post's seven-part series "Scammed."
"It was his voice." In late 2025, a Ponte Vedra grandmother lost $200,000 — money she had saved for years for the care of her autistic grandson — to a caller using what she swore was his voice. Reported by Action News Jax.

Look at who these victims are: a therapist, a devoted grandmother. Intelligence is not what these operations exploit. Love and urgency are. Everyone has both.

7. The first ten seconds — a script for any suspicious call

Say this, word for word, and stop talking:
"I don't handle anything like this over the phone. Goodbye."

Then hang up. If it might have been real, it will still be real in ten minutes — so now: (1) call the company or agency back on the number printed on your card, your statement, or their official website (never the number that just called you); (2) if it was "family," call that person on the number you already have, or ask for the safe word; (3) tell one other person what just happened. Scams live on secrecy and speed. Those three steps remove both.

What you never owe a caller: a name confirmation, a code, your date of birth, secrecy from your own family, staying on the line, or a decision made today. Real institutions always let you call back. Only a scammer needs you not to.

8. The family safe word

Pick one unguessable word as a family — nothing findable online, not a pet's name or the street you grew up on — write it on the rules card below, and agree on the rule: any call about trouble or money gets the safe word, or it gets a hang-up and a call back. No exceptions, no matter how real the voice sounds. It takes ninety seconds to set up and it defeats voice cloning completely: machines can copy a voice, but they cannot know the word.

Part 2 — The news that isn't

The second half of this guide matters just as much. Manipulation does not only arrive as a phone call — much of it now arrives dressed as news, and getting fooled by it is not a character flaw. It is the engineered outcome of a very well-funded machine, and everyone with a phone is standing inside it, your kids included.

9. Listen: a fifty-second newscast that never happened

"Top story tonight…" AI DEMO

What it says: leaked CDC documents, a scrapped food-safety program, closed-door meetings with lobbyists, a senator calling it the "biggest betrayal of public trust in a generation." None of it happened. No documents, no meetings, no senator. But notice the borrowed clothing: a real institution, an uncheckable "leak," that honest-sounding hedge — "which this station could not independently verify" — a quote from a person who is never named, and a promise to "keep following the story." The anchor's voice is AI. Fabricated stories wear the costume of real journalism because we are all trained to trust the costume.

10. How big the machine actually is

NewsGuard, a company whose full-time job is tracking the reliability of news sources, is currently monitoring 3,749 websites that look like news outlets but are generated entirely by AI — in 16 languages, publishing dozens of invented or laundered articles a day, under generic trustworthy names like "Times Business News." They exist to farm the ads on the pages you visit. Deepfaked newsreaders are already in commercial use by criminals: in one German case, scammers cloned two of the country's best-known TV anchors to sell fake investments. And on the question of who spreads all this — an MIT study of all 126,000 verified true and false news stories passed around Twitter over a decade found that falsehood travels significantly farther, faster, deeper, and more broadly than the truth, and that it is mostly humans, not bots, who do the spreading. We forward the exciting lie; the boring truth waits.

11. The outrage engine underneath it

Why is your feed full of this? Not because anyone sat down to deceive you personally. Because of a plain business decision: the platforms are paid for your attention, and outrage holds attention longest. Researchers at Yale, Northwestern, and elsewhere have shown that content triggering anger and moral outrage gets shared dramatically more, and that the recommendation algorithms learn to serve you more of exactly that. Thousands of engineers, working exactly as intended. So the next time a post makes your blood boil — your anger is not an accident. It is the product. Someone, somewhere, profits from you being furious at people you will never meet.

12. Real or fake? Six headlines — you are the editor

Three of these headlines are real, documented, and linked to their source on reveal. Three were written by this page in the classic fake-news style. After you answer, you'll see the tells.

13. The six techniques — learn to name them

The Cambridge researchers behind the inoculation work found that disinformation, everywhere in the world, recycles a small set of techniques. Once you can name them, you start seeing them everywhere — and they lose most of their power. The names vary, but these six cover nearly everything:

Emotional language. Words chosen to make you furious or terrified before you've thought.
False dilemma. "Either you're with us or you hate us." Two options, both rigged.
Scapegoating. A complicated problem explained by one villainous group.
Impersonation. A fake expert, a fake insider, a borrowed uniform.
Discrediting. Attacking the person saying it so you never weigh what they said.
Trolling & manufactured consensus. Thousands of "people" agreeing with each other, on purpose, to make a fringe claim look universal. It is a purchasable service.

Practice round: four short posts below. Tap the technique each one is using.

14. Spin: the headline can be true and still lie to you

The hardest kind, and the most important to understand. Not everything misleading is false. Try this:

One factual event. In June, a town installed six speed cameras on Main Street. In the following months, crashes fell by 20%, and the cameras brought in $400,000 in fines, which went into the town's general fund. Every word of that is true.

Headline A: "New cameras cut crashes 20% on Main Street"
Headline B: "Town pockets $400,000 from drivers with new Main Street cameras"

Both headlines are completely true. Both are also manipulations. Same facts, opposite impressions. A framed you as safer; B framed you as fleeced — nothing was invented, only selected. This is called spin, and it is the most common manipulation in respectable outlets, not just fake ones. The tell: when a story hands you a strong emotion along with the facts, ask what the same facts would look like framed the opposite way. Also watch for real footage with a false caption — old riot video presented as today's, a crowd clip from one country labeled as another. Real pictures, false story.

15. Check anything in under a minute — with tools you already have

You do not need special software. Before believing or forwarding anything that moved you:

1. Search the exact claim. If a real story is big enough to matter, more than one outlet with named reporters is on it. Nobody carrying it, or only a site you've never heard of? Stop there.
2. Read past the first paragraph of any result. Ask: who wrote this, what do they want me to feel, and do they link to any original document I could check?
3. Check the date. Enormous amounts of viral content is old material re-captioned for a new outrage.
4. Still unsure? Send it to someone you trust before you forward it. (That is rule 5 on the card.) Checking first is not slow — forwarding a lie to fifty people is what takes up your week.

16. The one question that cuts through everything

"Who benefits if I believe this — or get angry about it?" If the honest answer is "definitely not me," you have your answer about what to do next.

Part 3 — The habits

17. The five rules — one card for the fridge

1. Any call about money, bail, codes, or trouble: hang up. Call back on the number you already have.
2. A family member in trouble on the phone? Ask the safe word. No safe word — hang up, call them yourself.
3. Never read, forward, or type a text-message code for anyone. That code is the key to your account.
4. Gift cards are for gifts. Anyone who asks to be paid in them is a criminal, full stop.
5. A story that makes you furious or terrified? Don't forward it yet. Check it — or send it to someone you trust first.

Print it: the one-page Family Rules Card (PDF), with a line for your family's safe word. Tape it near the phone. — (The card was built to be printed in black and white, Letter size.)

18. Protect the raw material

Cloning needs samples. You cannot erase yourself from the internet, but you can make yourself an expensive target: keep social accounts private where possible, think twice before posting videos with clear speech of you or your grandchildren (a few seconds is enough), set your voicemail greeting to the default robotic one instead of your own voice, and never answer "get to know you" quizzes online — your first pet, your mother's maiden name, your childhood street are passwords to half your accounts. And please know: doing none of this perfectly is fine. The rules in section 17 work even when a scammer has your voice, your photos, and your grandson's name — because they never rely on recognizing the fake.

19. If it happens anyway

Someday one of these may get through — it gets through to people who run banks. What you do in the next hour matters far more than the mistake:

Move fast. Call your bank first — transfers can sometimes be frozen or recalled for a short window, and every hour counts. Then report it: reportfraud.ftc.gov and the FBI's ic3.gov. If it was gift cards, call the card company's number on the back of the card — funds still on the card can sometimes be recovered.
Tell the family immediately. The FBI's $5-billion figure exists only because most victims report. Shame protects exactly one party in this story, and it is not you. Talking about it the same day is how the next family gets warned before the same script reaches them.

20. A note for the kids and grandkids reading this

If you are the one who sent your parents this page: bring it up gently. Nobody — at any age — learns well after being made to feel stupid. Do not audit their phone habits or quiz them. Instead, do the quiz together, play them the fake calls, tell them a close call of your own, and set up the safe word as a family ritual rather than a lecture. Offer yourself as the "check first" person and mean it patiently for years: the hundredth forwarded article deserves the same quick, kind answer as the first.
